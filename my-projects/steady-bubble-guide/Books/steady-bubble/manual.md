# Administration

## Purpose and Scope

This document defines the standard operating procedures for NATO submarine operations conducted under STEADY BUBBLE tasking. It covers vessel specifications, shipboard systems, weapons employment, damage control, and threat assessment for all submarine classes assigned to this operational theatre.

All procedures assume Cold War-era rules of engagement against Soviet naval forces in open-ocean and littoral environments.

This manual consolidates operating procedures for five NATO submarine classes: Los Angeles (688i), Trafalgar, Swiftsure, Seawolf, and Type 209. Where procedures differ between nuclear and diesel-electric propulsion, this is noted explicitly.

# Navigation

## Helm Orders — Speed

Speed is ordered via the Engine Order Telegraph or incremental controls. The submarine accelerates and decelerates according to a realistic time constant — approximately 45 seconds to close the gap between ordered and actual speed.

### Standard Engine Orders

| Order | Speed |
|---|---|
| AHEAD FLANK | 28 knots (maximum — significant noise penalty) |
| AHEAD FULL | 20 knots (standard transit speed) |
| AHEAD STANDARD | 14 knots (economical transit) |
| AHEAD SLOW | 7 knots (quiet patrol) |
| AHEAD CREEP | 3 knots (minimum steerage, near-silent) |
| ALL STOP | 0 knots |
| BACK SLOW | 5 knots astern |
| BACK FULL | 10 knots astern |
| BACK EMERGENCY | 18 knots astern |

### Speed Considerations

Speed is the primary determinant of acoustic signature and sonar effectiveness. Every tactical decision involves a speed trade-off:

- **3-5 knots:** Optimal patrol speed. Near-silent. Maximum passive sonar range. Sufficient steerage for depth control.
- **7-10 knots:** Moderate transit. Audible noise increase. Sonar degradation begins at 4 knots.
- **14-20 knots:** Fast transit. Significant self-masking. Passive sonar seriously degraded above 10 knots.
- **20+ knots:** Sprint. The submarine is effectively deaf. Use only for repositioning or evasion. Cavitation risk at shallow depth.

Damage to propulsion or control systems may impose speed restrictions. A reactor SCRAM halts steam generation for the main turbines, limiting the boat to 3 knots on the battery-powered emergency propulsion motor.

## Helm Orders — Depth

Depth is ordered in 10-metre increments. The depth control system uses hydroplanes and main ballast tank fill to achieve and maintain ordered depth.

### Depth Control Characteristics

- Hydroplanes require forward speed to be effective. Below 10 knots, plane authority degrades linearly. At zero speed, planes are ineffective and buoyancy alone controls depth.
- The depth controller uses a two-zone approach: full authority outside 15 metres of the target depth, proportional settle within 15 metres.
- Flooding, trim imbalance, and HPA depletion all affect depth control authority.

### Depth Envelope

Every vessel class has a defined depth envelope. Exceeding these limits produces increasingly serious consequences:

| Zone | Definition | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Safe Diving Depth (SDD)** | Maximum depth for routine operations | Normal operations |
| **Diving Limit (DL)** | Certified maximum depth — CO is personally accountable | Warning to Conn |
| **Design Depth (DD)** | Structural design limit — emergency use only | Formal warning, CO decision required |
| **Collapse Depth** | Hull begins structural deformation — seep flooding initiates | Progressive flooding cascade, hull creaking |
| **Crush Depth** | Catastrophic structural failure | Immediate loss of the boat with all hands |

**Critical note:** Crush depth is not fixed. Structural damage from weapon impacts reduces the effective crush depth. A boat with two serious hits may have its crush depth reduced to approximately 72% of the nominal value. The crew receives no precise indication of the new limit — hull creaking is the only warning.

## Waypoint Navigation

Waypoints are set by designating positions on the tactical display. The submarine turns toward each waypoint in sequence using speed-dependent turn rates:

- Maximum turn rate: 2.2 degrees per second at flank speed
- Minimum turn rate: 0.5 degrees per second at creep speed

Multiple waypoints may be queued. The submarine follows them in order and reports each waypoint reached. Manual course orders from the compass display clear the route.

## Emergency Manoeuvres

### Emergency Turn

Hard rudder. Executed to break the track of an incoming torpedo or to rapidly change heading.

- Duration: 2.2 seconds of hard-over rudder
- Cooldown: 8 seconds before the next emergency turn is available
- Noise: Generates a significant acoustic transient
- Wire consequence: All torpedo guidance wires are severed immediately
- Towed array: Damages or destroys the array if deployed

### Crash Dive

Emergency deep. Executed to rapidly gain depth — typically to cross below the thermal layer or to open vertical separation from a surface threat.

- Duration: 3.5 seconds of maximum dive rate
- Cooldown: 12 seconds
- Effect: Orders depth to current +600m, sets flank speed, slams planes to maximum dive angle
- Noise: Generates a large acoustic transient
- Wire consequence: All torpedo guidance wires severed
- Towed array: Damages or destroys the array if deployed

### Combined Manoeuvre Risk

Executing both emergency turn and crash dive within rapid succession at speeds above 20 knots creates a 45% probability of reactor SCRAM due to mechanical shock to the reactor control rod assembly. This risk must be weighed against the immediate threat.

# Propulsion

## Nuclear Propulsion

The 688i, Trafalgar, Swiftsure, and Seawolf classes are nuclear-powered. The reactor provides unlimited endurance at any depth with no requirement to surface for propulsion.

The ship's battery charges automatically when the reactor is online and drains only during a reactor SCRAM.

### Reactor SCRAM

A SCRAM is the emergency rapid insertion of all control rods, halting the fission chain reaction. The reactor does not shut down or go cold — it continues to produce significant thermal energy from fission product decay heat and remains fully dependent on the primary coolant system for continued cooling. A true cold shutdown is a controlled evolution taking days to weeks; a SCRAM is an emergency protective action measured in seconds.

What a SCRAM does is deny the reactor the ability to sustain the steam generation required to drive the main turbines. Propulsion is lost because there is no longer sufficient thermal output to produce steam at the rate the turbines demand — not because the reactor has ceased to function.

The reactor plant remains an active system throughout a SCRAM. Primary coolant must continue circulating. The pressuriser must maintain system pressure. Failure of these systems during a SCRAM is a compounding emergency.

Causes:

- **Coolant system failure** not corrected within the 45-second countdown
- **Mechanical shock** from combined emergency manoeuvres at high speed (45% probability)
- **Direct weapon impact** on reactor or primary coolant systems (8% probability per severe hit)

Effects of SCRAM:
- Propulsion limited to 3 knots — emergency propulsion motor only (battery-powered)
- Battery begins draining (the EPM runs on stored battery charge)
- 75-second restart window before the reactor can be brought back to power
- All active propulsion casualties (coolant leak, steam leak, turbine trip) are cleared by the rod insertion

A SCRAM at depth with active flooding is one of the most dangerous situations the boat can face. With only 3 knots available, plane authority is marginal and depth control relies almost entirely on buoyancy. The reactor plant continues to require cooling throughout — damage to primary coolant systems during a SCRAM creates a compounding emergency with no good options.

## Reactor Casualties

### Primary Coolant Leak

Triggered by sustained high-speed operations at depth. After 15 seconds of continuous flank-speed running, the risk of a coolant leak begins. The probability increases if the primary coolant system is already degraded (3x risk multiplier).

Once detected, a 45-second countdown begins. If the crew cannot repair the leak within this window, an automatic SCRAM occurs.

- **Crew repair at low speed** (below 1/3 max): 65% success probability
- **Crew repair at high speed:** 30% success probability
- **Timer acceleration:** At flank speed, the countdown runs 50% faster. At low speed, it runs 50% slower.

The recommended response is to immediately reduce speed to improve both repair probability and countdown rate.

### Steam Leak

Triggered by weapon impact when the main turbines or pressuriser are already in a degraded or worse state. 12% probability per hit under these conditions.

- Speed is restricted to 7 knots (diesel backup / EPM only)
- Auto-repairs in 30-60 seconds
- No crew intervention required

### Turbine Trip

Triggered by nearby explosions (15% probability per hit) or by sudden throttle changes exceeding 10 knots per second (20% probability).

- Speed restricted to 12 knots (partial turbine output)
- Auto-recovers in 20-30 seconds
- No crew intervention required

Avoid rapid speed changes during or immediately after combat to reduce turbine trip risk.

### Reactor Runaway

An uncontrolled power excursion. 8% probability on a severe hit to the reactor or primary coolant system. The runaway produces:

- A loud acoustic transient audible to enemies at approximately 30 km / 16 nm
- Significant suspicion increase in all nearby hostile units
- No direct damage to the boat — the safety systems contain the excursion

The primary danger is the acoustic signature, not the reactor event itself. All nearby enemies will have a bearing on the boat.

## Diesel-Electric Propulsion (Type 209)

The Type 209 operates on battery power when submerged. The battery drains proportionally to speed.

### Battery Endurance

| Speed | Approximate Endurance |
|---|---|
| 3 knots | ~40 minutes |
| 5 knots | ~24 minutes |
| 8 knots (maximum) | ~15 minutes |

When the battery is depleted, all propulsion ceases. The boat must surface or snorkel immediately.

Battery warnings are issued at 30%, 20%, and 10% remaining charge.

### Snorkel Operations

The diesel engines can only operate with an air supply. This requires raising the snorkel mast to a depth of 12 metres.

- **Snorkel noise:** Extremely high — one of the loudest signatures in the operational theatre
- **Speed while snorkelling:** Limited to 5 knots
- **Surface charge rate:** Fastest charging — battery recovers at approximately 0.5% per second
- **Snorkel charge rate:** Slower — approximately 0.3% per second

Snorkel operations should be conducted only when the area is confirmed clear of hostile contacts. The noise signature during snorkelling is sufficient to alert any enemy within passive sonar range.

### Dead Battery Procedures

If the battery reaches zero charge while submerged and not snorkelling:

- All propulsion ceases immediately
- The boat begins sinking (no plane authority without speed)
- Snorkel must be ordered immediately
- If depth exceeds snorkel mast limits, the situation may be unrecoverable

## Silent Running

Silent running reduces the boat's acoustic signature by approximately 45% by:

- Capping speed at 8 knots
- Shutting down non-essential machinery
- Restricting crew movement

Silent running is toggled via the control panel. It cannot be activated if the Control Room has been rendered non-functional.

Silent running does not eliminate noise — it reduces it. At 8 knots in silent running, the boat is still audible at moderate range. For minimum noise, combine silent running with creep speed (3 knots).

## Cavitation

Cavitation occurs when the propeller blade tips create localised low-pressure zones that cause dissolved gas to form bubbles. These bubbles collapse violently, producing a sharp acoustic signature.

The speed at which cavitation begins depends on depth — deeper water has higher ambient pressure, which delays onset:

| Vessel | Cavitation Onset (at reference depth) |
|---|---|
| Type 209 | 11 knots |
| Swiftsure | 17 knots |
| Dallas (688i) | 18 knots |
| Trafalgar | 20 knots |
| Connecticut (Seawolf) | 22 knots |

Cavitation adds a severe noise penalty. At shallow depth, even moderate speeds may produce cavitation. Deep operations significantly raise the cavitation threshold.

# Sonar Systems

## Hull-Mounted Array — Passive Detection

The primary sensor. A large-aperture hydrophone array mounted in the bow of the submarine, listening for sounds produced by other vessels.

### How It Works

Every vessel in the water produces noise from its propulsion plant, auxiliary machinery, and movement through the water. The hull array detects this noise and presents it to the sonar operator as bearing lines on the tactical display.

Detection depends on the balance between the target's noise and the submarine's own noise (self-masking), modified by range, geometry, and environmental conditions.

### Effective Range

| Condition | Approximate Range |
|---|---|
| Loud target, own ship quiet | ~28 km / 15 nm |
| Moderate target, patrol speed | ~15-20 km / 8-11 nm |
| Quiet target, own ship loud | < 5 km / 3 nm |
| Across thermal layer | Range reduced by approximately 40% |

Sonar system damage reduces these ranges proportionally.

### Speed and Self-Masking

The submarine's own flow noise masks incoming signals. This is the most important trade-off in submarine warfare.

| Own Speed | Sonar Effectiveness |
|---|---|
| 0-3 knots | Near-maximum passive range |
| 4-7 knots | Slight degradation beginning — still operationally effective |
| 8-10 knots | Noticeable reduction in detection range |
| 10-15 knots | Seriously degraded — close-range detections only |
| 15+ knots | Effectively deaf — 80-90% reduction in capability |

### Baffle Zone

The hull array has a blind sector astern, masked by the submarine's own propeller noise. This dead zone expands with speed:

- At rest: approximately ±15° from dead astern
- At speed: expands by approximately 1.5° per knot (maximum ±45°)

Contacts in the baffle zone cannot be detected by the hull array. The towed array partially compensates for this — see below.

### Watch Fatigue

Sonar operators on watch fatigue over time. At maximum fatigue, detection probability is reduced by up to 40% and bearing estimates become noisier. Rotating the watch resets fatigue but requires a handover period.

## Towed Array

A long, thin hydrophone array streamed behind the submarine on a cable. Provides significantly greater detection range than the hull array but introduces a bearing ambiguity.

### Deployment

The towed array must be deployed before it can provide data:

- Deployment time: 30 seconds to full operational capability
- A halfway progress report is issued at 15 seconds
- Maximum deployment speed: 12 knots — the array cannot be streamed above this speed

### Detection Range

| State | Range |
|---|---|
| Operational | ~45 km / 24 nm |
| Damaged | ~30 km / 16 nm |
| Destroyed | N/A — array lost |

### Bearing Ambiguity

The towed array cannot distinguish port from starboard. Every detection produces two bearing candidates — one real, one a mirror image reflected across the submarine's heading axis. Both appear on the tactical display as dashed bearing lines.

The hull array resolves this ambiguity. When the hull array independently detects the same contact, the system compares bearings and determines which towed candidate is correct. This requires 1-3 consistent hull-array bearings.

**Do not fire on an unresolved towed-array contact.** There is a 50% probability of sending the weapon in the wrong direction.

### Restrictions and Hazards

- **Speed limit:** Above 18 knots, the cable is stressed and the array degrades. Above 22 knots, the cable parts and the array is lost.
- **Emergency manoeuvres:** Emergency turns and crash dives damage or destroy the deployed array.
- **Dead cone:** ±28° from dead astern — no returns from this sector.
- **Type 209:** Does not carry a towed array.

### Recovery

The array may be retracted at any time. Retraction takes 20 seconds. A retracted array is protected from speed damage and emergency manoeuvre damage.

## Active Sonar

Active sonar transmits an acoustic pulse and listens for return echoes. It provides precise range and bearing data — the only shipboard system capable of direct range measurement at distance.

### Capabilities

- Detection range: approximately 18 km / 10 nm
- Provides direct range data (passive sonar provides only bearing)
- Cooldown: 9 seconds between transmissions
- Dazzle effect: disrupts enemy torpedo seekers within ~18 km for 1.5 seconds

### Cost

Active sonar is the loudest emission the submarine can produce. A single ping alerts every hostile unit within approximately 50 km / 27 nm. All alerted units receive:

- A precise bearing to the submarine
- A large suspicion increase

Active sonar should be reserved for situations where the tactical benefit outweighs the cost:

- Confirming range for a weapons solution when passive TMA is insufficient
- Dazzling incoming torpedo seekers as a last-resort defensive measure
- Locating contacts in a high-clutter environment where passive detection has failed

Routine use of active sonar is operationally inappropriate and likely to be fatal.

## Thermal Layer

A temperature gradient exists between 180m and 280m depth. This thermocline significantly affects acoustic propagation.

### Acoustic Effect

Sound crossing the thermal layer boundary loses approximately 40% of its signal strength. This applies equally to:

- The submarine's own noise reaching enemies
- Enemy noise reaching the submarine
- Torpedo seeker ranges when crossing the layer

### Tactical Significance

The thermal layer is the single most important environmental factor in submarine warfare.

**Defensive use:** Operating below the layer (deeper than 280m) while enemies patrol above it provides a significant acoustic advantage. Enemies must close to approximately 60% of their normal detection range to find the submarine.

**Offensive use:** Fire control solutions can be built from below the layer using passive sonar. Torpedoes can be wire-guided across the layer boundary — the wire provides guidance through the acoustic shadow zone until the seeker can acquire the target.

**Layer crossing:** When transiting through the thermocline (180-280m), the submarine is briefly detectable from both sides. Cross quickly and at minimum noise.

## Convergence Zone

At distances of approximately 48-55 km / 26-30 nm from the submarine, acoustic energy refracts through the deep water column and converges, creating a detection annulus at extreme range.

### Conditions

CZ detection requires both platforms to be below the thermal layer. If either platform is above the layer, CZ propagation does not occur.

### Characteristics

- Signal boost: approximately 3.2x compared to direct-path at the same range
- Useful for early warning of approaching threat groups
- Bearing accuracy is lower than direct-path detection due to the refraction geometry

CZ detections appear as normal bearing lines on the tactical display. The extreme range means bearing rate is very slow — TMA takes significantly longer to build at CZ distances.

# Target Motion Analysis

## Principles

TMA is the process of determining a contact's position, course, and speed from a series of passive bearing observations taken over time. It is the foundation of the fire control solution.

The sonar team triangulates the contact's position by comparing bearings taken from different positions as the submarine moves. The quality of the solution depends on three factors:

1. **Number of observations** — more bearings provide a better statistical fit. Six or more observations contribute full quality.
2. **Baseline** — the distance the submarine has moved between its first and most recent observation. Approximately 3.5 km / 2 nm of travel provides full baseline contribution.
3. **Bearing crossing angle** — the angular divergence between observations. This is the critical factor and can only be generated by changing course.

### The Manoeuvre Requirement

If the submarine maintains a constant heading, all bearing observations are nearly parallel. The TMA solver cannot triangulate from parallel lines — quality caps at approximately 28%, well below the firing threshold.

A course change of at least 15-20° is required to generate useful bearing crossing. A change of 30°+ produces rapid quality improvement. Two changes in opposite directions (a zig-zag) produce optimal geometry.

## Solution Quality

| Quality | Classification | Capability |
|---|---|---|
| Below 15% | No solution | Bearing line only — observation continues |
| 15% - 35% | Position estimate | Approximate position shown on display — not sufficient for weapons |
| 35% - 70% | DEGRADED | Range estimate available. Weapons may be fired with wire guidance only — no lead-angle computation. Suitable for close-range engagements. |
| Above 70% | SOLID | Full solution. Weapons are fired with lead-angle intercept — the wire steers the torpedo ahead of the target's projected position. Required for long-range engagements. |

### Solution Decay

If observations cease (the contact goes quiet or the submarine loses detection), TMA quality degrades over time. A SOLID solution degrades to DEGRADED in approximately 25-30 seconds without fresh bearings. Regaining quality requires new observations and possibly fresh manoeuvring.

## Contact Classification

As TMA quality builds, the sonar team progressively classifies the contact based on acoustic analysis:

### Stage 1 — Broadband Hull Type (Quality ≥ 20%)

Initial classification from broadband noise characteristics:
- SUBMERGED — submarine contact
- SURFACE — surface vessel
- MERCHANT — commercial vessel (non-combatant)

### Stage 2 — Tonal Analysis (Quality ≥ 35%, plus 15-25 seconds accumulation)

Narrowband frequency analysis identifies the general platform type:
- SSN, SSK, SSBN, SSGN — submarine categories
- FRIGATE, DESTROYER, CORVETTE, CRUISER — surface combatant categories

### Stage 3 — Machinery Signature (Quality ≥ 50%, plus 20-40 seconds accumulation)

Detailed machinery analysis identifies the specific class:
- SSN AKULA, SSK KILO, FRIGATE KRIVAK, etc.

Classification requires sustained quality above the threshold. If TMA quality drops below the required level, the accumulation timer resets.

## Fire Control — Torpedo Data Computer

The TDC computes the fire solution from TMA data and generates the wire guidance commands that steer the torpedo to the target.

### Designating a Target

A sonar contact is designated by selecting it on the tactical display. The TDC then displays the current solution quality and bearing data for the designated contact.

### Solution Requirements

- **Minimum quality for firing:** DEGRADED (35%)
- **At DEGRADED:** Wire steers toward the contact's latest bearing. No lead-angle prediction. Effective at close range.
- **At SOLID (70%+):** Wire computes a lead angle using bearing rate and estimated range. The torpedo is steered ahead of the target's projected position. Required for reliable hits at long range.

### Firing Conditions

Before a weapon can be launched:

- At least one torpedo tube must be loaded and ready
- The submarine's speed must be at or below 15 knots (wire constraint)
- The target bearing must be within ±55° of the submarine's heading (firing arc)
- No other weapons must be in the firing sequence (one launch at a time)

### Fire Delay

There is a 4.5-second procedural delay between the fire order and weapon launch. During this time the crew floods the tube, opens outer doors, and fires. This delay is realistic and cannot be shortened.

## Wire Guidance

All torpedoes are wire-guided from launch. A thin guidance wire connects the torpedo to the submarine's fire control system, allowing the TDC to steer the weapon toward the target.

### Wire Constraints

| Condition | Effect |
|---|---|
| Speed ≤ 15 knots | No wire stress — normal operations |
| Speed 15-20 knots | Wire under stress — parts in approximately 25 seconds |
| Speed 20-22 knots | Severe stress — parts in approximately 3 seconds |
| Speed > 22 knots | Wire parts immediately |
| Emergency turn | All wires severed immediately |
| Crash dive | All wires severed immediately |
| Wire range exceeded (~30 km / 16 nm) | Wire parts automatically |

### Wire-to-Seeker Handoff

When the torpedo approaches the target and its onboard seeker detects the target within its acquisition cone, the seeker takes over guidance. The wire is no longer needed — the torpedo homes autonomously.

At SOLID solution quality, the wire steers the torpedo to an intercept point ahead of the target. At DEGRADED, it steers toward the last known bearing. SOLID solutions produce dramatically higher hit rates at range.

### Multiple Wires

Multiple torpedoes can be in the water simultaneously, each on its own wire. The fire control system steers each independently toward its designated target. The speed constraint applies to all wires collectively — exceeding 15 knots stresses all live wires.

### Manual Wire Control

Individual torpedo wires can be manually controlled:

- **Auto TDC mode** (default): Fire control steers the weapon automatically
- **Manual bearing override:** The operator can nudge the torpedo bearing in degree increments
- **Wire cut:** The wire can be deliberately severed, releasing the torpedo to its seeker or search pattern

## Anti-Ship Cruise Missiles

ASCMs are effective against surface warships. They are ineffective against submarines.

### Missile Types

| Missile | Speed | Range | Warhead | Max Launch Depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UGM-84 Harpoon | ~830 km/h | ~250 km / 135 nm | High | 25m |
| UGM-84 Sub-Harpoon | ~830 km/h | ~250 km / 135 nm | High | 25m |
| BGM-109C TASM | ~740 km/h | Unlimited | Very High | 30m |
| SM39 Exocet | ~685 km/h | ~90 km / 49 nm | Moderate | 55m |

### Launch Procedures

- The submarine must be at or shallower than the missile's maximum launch depth
- A surface contact must be designated
- The missile ascends for approximately 2 seconds, transitions to cruise flight, and flies to the designated target area
- The onboard seeker activates at approximately 8 km from the waypoint and scans for surface targets
- If no target is acquired within 8 seconds, the missile is lost

### Launch Depth Considerations

Harpoon and Sub-Harpoon require the submarine to be at 25m or shallower — just below periscope depth. This exposes the boat to surface detection.

TASM fires from VLS cells at up to 30m — slightly more margin.

SM39 Exocet (Type 209 only) can launch from 55m — the deepest launch capability of any ASCM in this inventory. This significantly reduces exposure risk.

## Countermeasures

### Noisemaker Decoy

The primary defensive countermeasure against incoming torpedoes.

- **Stock:** 12 per mission
- **Cooldown:** 4.5 seconds between deployments
- **Duration:** 14-20 seconds active life
- **Function:** Produces a loud acoustic signature that competes with the submarine for the torpedo's seeker attention

### Effectiveness

Countermeasure effectiveness depends critically on the submarine's own noise level at the moment of deployment:

| Submarine Noise Level | Seduction Probability |
|---|---|
| Very quiet (creep + silent running) | ~90% |
| Quiet (slow patrol) | ~75% |
| Moderate (normal patrol) | ~64% |
| Loud (moderate speed) | ~43% |
| Minimum floor (regardless of noise) | 15% |

The quieter the submarine at the moment of countermeasure deployment, the more likely the torpedo will be seduced away. This is because the decoy must acoustically outcompete the submarine — if the submarine is louder than the decoy, the torpedo may ignore the decoy.

### Post-Seduction Behaviour

After a torpedo has been seduced and the decoy expires:

- The torpedo enters a reacquisition delay (4-8 seconds depending on torpedo type)
- It then executes a hook manoeuvre: breaks 45° away from the decoy's last position, hooks 125° back to cross the original target track
- It then resumes a search snake pattern

This reacquisition delay is the window in which the submarine must open range, change depth, or cross the thermal layer.

# Damage Control

## Hull Structure

The submarine hull is divided into six watertight sections (WTS), separated by watertight doors. Each section contains multiple compartments across three internal decks.

| WTS | Designation | Complement | Primary Systems |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fore Ends (Torpedo Room) | 23 | Torpedo tubes, sonar array, TMA computer, forward planes |
| 2 | Control Room | 20 | Helm, fire control, ballast control, periscope, navigation |
| 3 | Mess Decks (Auxiliary) | 7 | CO2 scrubbers, O2 generator, auxiliary power, sickbay |
| 4 | Reactor Compartment | 3 | Reactor, primary coolant, pressuriser, radiation monitoring |
| 5 | Engine Room (Maneuvering) | 20 | Propulsion, main turbines, electrical distribution, emergency diesel |
| 6 | Aft Ends (Engineering) | 15 | Towed array, steering gear, aft planes, shaft seals, escape trunk |

Total complement: approximately 88 personnel.

Some compartments within each section are unmanned during normal operations. Casualties in unmanned compartments (particularly fire) may go undetected for 30-60 seconds until sensor alarms trigger or watchkeepers notice secondary indications.

## Flooding

### Cause

Torpedo impacts breach the hull. The rate and severity of flooding depend on the hit location, explosive yield, and current depth (deeper water = higher ambient pressure = faster flooding).

### Effects

Flooding produces cascading effects:

- **Buoyancy loss:** Each fully flooded section adds significant negative buoyancy. The submarine sinks faster as more sections flood.
  - One section flooded: controllable with depth planes at moderate speed
  - Two sections flooded: approximately 2 m/s sink rate. Planes may not hold without emergency blow.
  - Three sections flooded: approximately 3 m/s sink rate. The boat is almost certainly lost.
- **Trim imbalance:** Forward or aft flooding creates a pitch moment that the depth planes must compensate for. At low speed, plane authority may be insufficient.
- **System damage:** Equipment in flooded compartments degrades through offline to destroyed as water rises.
- **Crew casualties:** Personnel in flooded sections are killed or injured.
- **Acoustic penalty:** Bilge pumps and drainage systems add to the submarine's noise signature.

### Flooding Management

DC teams auto-dispatch to flooding casualties at emergency stations. The crew shores up breaches and operates pumps to slow or halt the flooding.

The commanding officer's primary role during a flooding casualty is:

1. Maintain adequate speed for plane authority
2. Monitor depth — arrest any descent before reaching the collapse envelope
3. Consider emergency blow if flooding rate exceeds plane compensation
4. Manage the boat's noise signature — pumps are loud, but sinking is worse

## Fire

### Cause

Fires start from torpedo impacts, system failures, electrical faults, or cascading damage from adjacent fires.

### Detection

- **Manned compartments:** Fire is reported immediately by the crew on station.
- **Unmanned compartments:** Fire burns undetected for the compartment-specific detection delay (typically 30-60 seconds). Detection occurs when sensor alarms trigger or smoke reaches a manned space.

### Effects

- Fire intensity increases over time if unchecked
- Fire spreads to adjacent compartments within the same watertight section
- Equipment in burning compartments degrades as fire intensity rises
- Personnel in compartments with severe fire suffer casualties
- A major fire produces toxic gases that affect adjacent spaces

### Fire Management

DC teams fight fires with portable extinguishers and the fixed drench system. Priority is:

1. Isolate the affected section (close WTDs if not already closed)
2. Fight the fire directly (DC team dispatched)
3. Activate the drench system for severe fires
4. Evacuate crew from affected compartments if fire intensity threatens personnel

## Damage Control Teams

Two DC teams are embarked:

| Team | Designation | Muster Location | Area of Responsibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | Forward DC Party | SR Mess (WTS 3) | Primary response — nearest casualty |
| Bravo | Aft DC Party | Aft Passage (WTS 5) | Secondary response — second casualty or distant section |

### Operating Procedures

1. **Muster.** On emergency stations, teams muster at their designated locations. This takes 10-15 seconds.
2. **Dispatch.** Teams auto-dispatch to the highest-priority casualty: flooding takes precedence over fire, which takes precedence over system repair.
3. **Transit.** Teams move through the boat to reach the casualty location. Transit time ranges from 10 seconds (adjacent section) to 56 seconds (opposite end of the boat).
4. **Work.** Teams conduct damage control at the casualty site — shoring breaches, fighting fires, or repairing systems.

### Transit Constraints

- **Watertight doors:** Closed WTDs block crew transit between sections (except through the reactor tunnel bypass between WTS 3/4 and WTS 4/5).
- **Flooded sections:** Teams may need to transit through partially flooded sections at risk.
- **Reactor compartment:** The reactor tunnel bypass allows transit through WTS 4 regardless of door state — crew do not enter the reactor space itself.

DC teams operate autonomously at emergency stations. The commanding officer does not issue direct orders to DC teams.

## Watertight Doors

Five WTDs separate adjacent watertight sections. They are operated hydraulically from the main hydraulic plant (Control Room).

| Door | Between |
|---|---|
| WTD 1 | WTS 1 (Fore Ends) — WTS 2 (Control Room) |
| WTD 2 | WTS 2 (Control Room) — WTS 3 (Mess Decks) |
| WTD 3 | WTS 3 (Mess Decks) — WTS 4 (Reactor Comp) |
| WTD 4 | WTS 4 (Reactor Comp) — WTS 5 (Engine Room) |
| WTD 5 | WTS 5 (Engine Room) — WTS 6 (Aft Ends) |

### Door States

- **Open:** Normal condition. Crew can transit freely. Flooding and fire spread unimpeded.
- **Closed:** Flooding and fire spread between sections is slowed. Crew transit is blocked (except reactor tunnel bypass for WTDs 3 and 4).

### Hydraulic Dependency

WTD operation requires the main hydraulic plant (located in WTS 2, Control Room). If the hydraulic plant is destroyed, doors cannot be operated.

### Reactor Tunnel Bypass

WTDs adjacent to the reactor compartment (WTD 3 and WTD 4) have a bypass tunnel through the reactor D1 top deck passage. Crew transit through these doors is never blocked, even when the doors are closed. This ensures DC teams can always reach the aft sections of the boat.

## Shipboard Systems

Every major system is tracked individually and can be in one of four states:

| State | Operational Effect | Repair Time |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal | Full capability | — |
| Degraded | Reduced capability — system-specific effects | ~20 seconds |
| Offline | System non-functional | ~45 seconds |
| Destroyed | Non-functional — longest repair time | ~120 seconds |

| System | Location | Effect When Degraded/Offline |

| Torpedo Tubes | WTS 1 | Reduced tube availability / offline = no firing |

### Control Node Dependencies

Some systems depend on a control node. If the control node is degraded or worse, the dependent system's effective state is the worst of its own state and the control node's state.

- Torpedo tubes depend on fire control
- Forward planes depend on helm
- TDC depends on fire control

## Crew

### Watch System

The crew is divided into Watch A and Watch B. One watch is on duty while the other rests.

- **Fatigue:** The on-watch crew fatigues over time, degrading sonar detection probability by up to 40%.
- **Watch change:** Rotating the watch resets fatigue. There is a brief handover period during the transition.
- **Action stations:** Both watches are called to stations during combat. This doubles available crew but accelerates fatigue.

### Casualties

Personnel are killed or injured by flooding, fire, and weapon explosions.

- **Injured:** Receive medical treatment in sickbay (WTS 3). May return to duty.
- **Killed:** Permanently lost. No replacement available.

Loss of crew in a section means:

- Systems in unmanned compartments go unmonitored
- Fire detection in those compartments is delayed
- Damage reports from that section cease
- The section's contribution to ship operations is reduced

# High-Pressure Air

## System Overview

HPA is the compressed air system that provides the energy for three critical functions:

1. **Ballast tank control** — adjusting main ballast tank fill to control buoyancy and depth
2. **Torpedo launch** — impulse air to eject weapons from torpedo tubes
3. **Emergency blow** — forcing water out of ballast tanks to arrest an uncontrolled descent

## Pressure Banks

| Bank | Capacity | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Group Bank | 207 bar | Normal operations: ballast control, torpedo launch |
| Reserve Bank | 207 bar | Emergency use only. Isolated until committed during emergency blow. |

### Pressure vs Depth

Ambient water pressure increases with depth at approximately 1 bar per 10 metres. HPA pressure must exceed ambient pressure by a factor of 1.2 for full ballast control authority.

| Depth | Ambient Pressure | HPA Required for Full Authority |
|---|---|---|
| 100m | 10 bar | 12 bar |
| 200m | 20 bar | 24 bar |
| 300m | 30 bar | 36 bar |
| 400m | 40 bar | 48 bar |

If HPA pressure drops below the required threshold, the submarine cannot drain its ballast tanks — it can flood them (increase negative buoyancy) but cannot empty them (increase positive buoyancy). This is a critical constraint during deep operations and after sustained combat.

### Consumption

- **Ballast adjustments:** HPA is consumed during normal depth changes as air is used to displace water from the tanks
- **Torpedo launch:** Each torpedo launch consumes approximately 2 bar from the group bank
- **Emergency blow:** Consumes air at a rate proportional to the pressure differential — rapid consumption at depth

### Recharge

HPA can only be recharged at or near the surface (20m or shallower):

- **LP compressor:** Slow recharge, operates silently and continuously at the surface
- **HP compressor:** Fast recharge, operator-initiated, generates significant noise

The reserve bank recharges at a reduced rate compared to the group bank.

## Emergency Blow

Emergency blow is the last-resort procedure for arresting an uncontrolled descent. It vents high-pressure air directly into the main ballast tanks, forcing water out and creating positive buoyancy.

### Procedure

1. Emergency blow is ordered from the command panel
2. The system checks HPA pressure against ambient. If the group bank alone is insufficient, the reserve bank is committed
3. If total available pressure is below ambient, the blow fails — there is insufficient air to displace the water
4. If the ballast control system is damaged, the automatic blow mechanism fails. DC teams operate the valves manually, adding approximately 12 seconds of delay
5. Air flows into the tanks at a rate proportional to the pressure differential
6. Tanks are considered clear when average fill drops below 2%

### Cancellation

Emergency blow is cancelled by:

- Issuing any new depth order (this is an important caution — do not adjust depth controls during a blow unless intentionally cancelling)
- The submarine reaching 20m or shallower (surface/near-surface)

### Post-Blow

After tanks clear, the submarine becomes positively buoyant and rises. At or near the surface:

- HPA recharges automatically
- The submarine is exposed to radar and visual detection
- Surface threats may engage with gunfire or missiles

# Masts and Sensors

## Periscope

The attack periscope provides visual observation of the surface environment.

### Operating Parameters

- **Safe operating depth:** 22m or shallower
- **Crush depth:** 40m — the mast is destroyed if raised below this depth
- **Raise/lower time:** 4 seconds each
- **Exposure duration:** 4.5 seconds per observation

### Tactical Considerations

Raising the periscope provides a detection advantage — enemy contacts within approximately 36 km / 19 nm are detected at enhanced sensitivity (1.55x normal).

However, the raised periscope creates a visual and radar signature that exposes the submarine's position to any enemy within approximately 36 km / 19 nm.

The periscope should be used in brief, deliberate observations — raise, observe, lower. Extended exposure increases the risk of counter-detection.

UK vessels carry an additional search periscope with a 5-second raise/lower time.

## Electronic Support Measures (ESM)

ESM passively detects electromagnetic emissions (radar, radio) from other platforms.

- **Range:** Approximately 120 km / 65 nm
- **Operating depth:** 22m or shallower (mast must be raised)
- **Emission:** None — ESM is entirely passive and does not reveal the submarine's position

ESM is the safest mast to raise. It provides long-range awareness of surface threats without any emission risk. The primary limitation is the requirement to be at shallow depth.

## Radar

Active surface-search radar.

- **Range:** Approximately 70 km / 38 nm
- **Operating depth:** 22m or shallower
- **Emission:** Active — the radar signal is detectable by enemy ESM systems

Radar provides excellent surface awareness but reveals the submarine's presence to any platform with ESM capability. Use with caution and only when the tactical situation warrants active emission.

## Communications

The communications mast enables long-range radio communications.

- **Operating depth:** 22m or shallower
- **Raise/lower time:** 3 seconds

# Acoustic Signature

## Signature Composition

The submarine's acoustic signature is the sum of all noise sources. A higher signature means easier detection by enemy platforms.

### Noise Sources

| Source | Contribution | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Machinery baseline | Vessel-specific (very low to low) | Always present — determined by hull design and propulsion type |
| Flow noise | Proportional to speed | The dominant noise source at transit speeds |
| Propeller tonals | Increases with turn rate | Turning generates additional blade-rate harmonics |
| Flank speed | Large additional penalty | Running at maximum speed produces significantly more noise than full speed |
| Cavitation | Severe penalty when active | Occurs at speed thresholds that vary by depth and vessel |
| Transients | Decays over time | Produced by course changes, emergency manoeuvres, weapon launches |
| Auxiliary machinery | Varies | HP compressor, flooding pumps, snorkel operations |

### Signature Reduction

- **Silent running:** Reduces total signature by approximately 45% (speed limited to 8 knots)
- **Reduced speed:** The most effective single measure. Each knot reduction lowers flow noise.
- **Depth:** Operating deeper reduces cavitation risk. Has no direct effect on machinery noise.
- **Avoid transients:** Smooth course and speed changes. Avoid emergency manoeuvres unless necessary.

## Enemy Detection Thresholds

The submarine's noise level determines whether enemies can detect it and how quickly their suspicion rises:

- **Very low noise** (creep + silent running): Detectable only at close range. Enemy suspicion decays at an accelerated rate.
- **Low noise** (slow patrol): Detectable at moderate range. Enemy suspicion decays normally.
- **Moderate noise** (standard transit): Detectable at long range. Enemy suspicion builds on detection.
- **High noise** (flank speed / snorkel): Detectable at maximum passive range by all enemies in the area. Enemy suspicion rises rapidly.

## Enemy Doctrine

### Detection

Soviet submarines use passive sonar with shorter effective range and wider baffle zones than NATO equivalents. Soviet machinery is louder than NATO equivalents, giving them a higher noise floor:

- Soviet submarine noise: significantly louder than NATO boats
- Soviet passive sonar range: shorter than NATO equivalent
- Soviet speed deafness: begins at lower speeds than NATO systems

### Prosecution

Enemy contacts are prosecuted through a suspicion-based system:

1. **Investigation:** On initial detection, the enemy manoeuvres toward the datum to establish contact.
2. **Prosecution:** As suspicion rises, the enemy transitions to active prosecution — positioning for a weapons solution.
3. **Engagement:** At high suspicion, the enemy fires torpedoes.

### Wolfpack Coordination

Soviet submarines operating within approximately 35 km / 19 nm of each other share tactical data. When one unit detects the NATO submarine, nearby units receive:

- Bearing to the contact
- Approximate position estimate
- Suspicion state information

This enables coordinated prosecution from multiple directions simultaneously. The pinger role is specifically designed to fix the target's position for the group.

### ASW Surface Ship Doctrine

Surface warships follow a structured ASW prosecution:

- **Active sonar prosecution:** Initiated when suspicion exceeds a moderate threshold
- **Hull sonar:** Effective range approximately 16 km / 9 nm. Limited to above the thermal layer.
- **Variable Depth Sonar (VDS):** Extended range approximately 24 km / 13 nm. Can detect below the thermal layer — the VDS is lowered beneath the thermocline.
- **ASROC missile torpedo:** A rocket-delivered torpedo that splashes into the water near the estimated contact position and begins a circular search pattern. Range approximately 3-28 km / 1.6-15 nm.

### Baffle Clearing

Certain enemy submarine types (hunter, interceptor, and AKULA-class) periodically execute baffle-clearing manoeuvres — turning 35° off their base heading to listen into their stern dead zone. This occurs every 90-150 seconds and the cleared heading is held for 20-30 seconds.

This means contacts in the enemy's baffles are periodically exposed to detection. Do not assume the baffle zone provides permanent concealment.

### Adaptation

Enemies learn from missed engagements. Each torpedo that fails to hit tightens their subsequent fire control accuracy by approximately 15% (to a floor of 50% of original error). This adaptation resets if:

- 60 seconds pass without another engagement
- The target changes heading by more than 30°

Repeated engagements against the same enemy group without repositioning become progressively more dangerous.

## Soviet Torpedo Characteristics

Soviet torpedoes are measurably inferior to NATO weapons across all parameters:

| Parameter | NATO MK-48 ADCAP | Soviet SET-65 / TEST-71 |
|---|---|---|
| Run speed | 50 knots | 40 knots |
| Seeker range | ~5 km / 2.8 nm | ~3.4 km / 1.8 nm |
| Reacquisition reliability | High | Low |
| Endurance | 210 seconds | 180 seconds |

The speed differential is the most significant tactical factor. NATO torpedoes outrun Soviet torpedoes — a Soviet torpedo fired at a submarine running at 20+ knots may not have sufficient speed advantage to close the range before its fuel is exhausted.

Soviet torpedo countermeasure resistance is lower than NATO equivalents. Noisemaker decoys are more effective against Soviet weapons than against NATO weapons.
